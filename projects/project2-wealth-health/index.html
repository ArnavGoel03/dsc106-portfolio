Proposition: National wealth is a reliable predictor of better health.

We measure national wealth as GDP per capita in current US dollars (NY.GDP.PCAP.CD) and health as life expectancy at birth (SP.DYN.LE00.IN), both from the World Bank World Development Indicators for 2023, the latest year with near complete coverage. Regional and income aggregates are excluded so that every mark is a single country or territory. Of the 217 economies in the database, 13 have no 2023 GDP figure and are dropped from both charts (among them Cuba, Eritrea, North Korea, South Sudan, Syria and Yemen), which leaves 204. All charts on this page are produced by make_charts.py, which fetches the data from the World Bank API and caches it locally.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • −0.5 Binning countries into equal-count GDP quintiles. Initially, our checkpoint paired a log-scale scatter plot with this box plot, but with more than 200 countries the scatter plot suffered from severe overplotting. Grouping countries into five bins of about 41 each removes the overplotting and turns a noisy cloud into a clean staircase, and the boxes still show the spread within each group. However, equal-count bins weight every country equally regardless of population, and the richest fifth spans $36K to $257K, so the flattening of the curve among wealthy countries is folded into a single box where the reader cannot see it. Because that flattening is exactly the evidence against the proposition (it is the subject of our AGAINST chart), we score this choice as mildly deceptive.
  • +0.5 Showing outliers as small, faint, unlabelled dots. Countries beyond 1.5 times the interquartile range are drawn as small grey dots, and the footnote explains them, so no data point is hidden. This matters most in the richest fifth, whose low dots include the United States, Saudi Arabia, Greenland, the Bahamas and Sint Maarten (with Monaco and San Marino above the box), and in the fourth fifth, where Nauru sits far below. We score this as only mildly earnest because the dots are deliberately quiet and unlabelled: a reader sees that exceptions exist but not that the most prominent one is the United States, which would invite the question of whether wealth alone explains health.
  • +1.5 Printing the medians and the gains between groups. Each box carries its median above the upper whisker, a row along the bottom labelled “Gain between fifths” gives the difference between adjacent medians (+6.7, +2.7, +3.7, +4.4), each group’s dollar range is printed under it, and a bracket summarises the total gap of 17.5 years. These are calculated fields that the reader can check directly against the boxes, so they persuade by making the size of the effect concrete rather than by distorting it.
  • −0.5 A connecting line and a y-axis starting at 50 years. A dashed line joins the five medians. A line between categorical groups is a mild departure from convention, and it invites the reader to see a single path that countries climb as they grow richer, which is a stronger story than a comparison of different countries in one year supports. Life expectancy has no meaningful zero, so a truncated axis is common practice; nevertheless, a floor of 50 places the lowest whisker just above the bottom and the richest box near the top, so the climb fills the whole plot. A lower floor, such as 40, would make the same differences look more modest.
  • +0.5 Title and subtitle framing. The title, “The richer the country, the longer its people live”, is an accurate description of the association across all 204 countries and territories, and the subtitle states plainly what is plotted, the number of countries and the year. We score it as mildly earnest rather than fully earnest because the phrasing reads naturally as cause and effect, and the chart offers no hint that the relationship weakens among rich countries.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • −1.5 Choosing which countries appear: the richest fifth, with at least one million people. Initially, our preferred AGAINST chart compared the change in life expectancy with the change in income, but its fitted slope was still positive, so it supported the proposition rather than opposing it. The final chart therefore builds on the “rich club” scatter plot from our checkpoint and shows only the range in which the relationship flattens. Rather than an arbitrary dollar cut-off, the scope is the richest of the same five groups used in the FOR chart, so it reads as a zoom into that chart’s top box. The World Bank’s own “high income” classification would have sounded even more neutral, but it begins near $14,000 and its members still show a clearly positive relationship (r = 0.55 with the same population filter), so it would not have served this side. Across all 204 countries and territories, the correlation between log GDP per capita and life expectancy is 0.86, and this chart never shows it. We then drop places with fewer than one million people, which we would justify on the grounds that life expectancy estimates for very small populations are unstable. It is worth mentioning that this removes 16 of the 41 members of the richest fifth, including Monaco, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg and Bermuda, which are among the richest and longest-lived places in the dataset; the FOR chart keeps them. With them included, the slope is +0.22 years per $10,000 and r = 0.35; with them removed, it falls to +0.14 and r to 0.19. This follows the feedback we received to remove countries so that the fit reflects the stance. Both rules are stated in the subtitle, but their effect on the slope is not.
  • +1 Median bars for each quarter of the countries. We split the 25 countries into four groups of six or seven by GDP per capita and drew a solid bar at the median life expectancy of each group, spanning that group’s income range, with the four medians printed in a row along the bottom (81.8, 81.6, 82.1 and 82.9 years). The bars form a visibly flat staircase even though income nearly triples from the first group to the last. This mirrors the quintile binning of the FOR chart, so the two arguments are directly comparable, and a median is robust to individual countries such as the United States. Within the countries shown, this is an honest summary; the deception lies in which countries are shown, which is scored separately above. The chart also uses one point per country for a single year, so all 25 points are visible and none overlap.
  • −0.5 Scale and unit choices. The x-axis is linear rather than logarithmic, which stretches the wealthiest countries far to the right and makes the distance travelled in income look large relative to the gain in years. The dashed least-squares line is kept, with its slope and correlation printed, so a reader can check the claim against the marks; however, we express the slope per $10,000, which makes the effect sound negligible, whereas across the full range of the chart it amounts to roughly one year. The y-axis runs from 75 to 90 years, which is a conventional range for these countries and does not by itself exaggerate the flatness.
  • −0.5 Selective labels. We labelled ten of the 25 countries: Saudi Arabia, Italy, Hong Kong, Germany, Qatar, Singapore, Norway, Switzerland, Ireland and the United States. Each label is accurate, all are in the same neutral ink, and several serve only as reference points across the income range. However, the set was chosen to support the stance: Italy and Hong Kong live long at moderate incomes for this group, whilst the United States, drawn as a slightly darker dot, is very rich but comparatively short-lived (78.4 years at $83K, against 85.2 in Hong Kong at $51K). The US gap is widely attributed to factors other than income, which the chart does not mention.
  • −0.5 Title and subtitle framing. The title, “In the richest fifth of countries, life expectancy levels off”, is calm and accurate for the countries shown, and the subtitle states the quintile, its dollar floor, the population filter and the year. We avoided charged wording so that the framing would not alert the reader. The deception is one of scope: a reader who sees this chart in a debate about whether wealth predicts health is likely to generalise from the plateau to the whole world, which the title does nothing to prevent.

Final Reflection

The FOR side was the more straightforward of the two, because the data genuinely support it: across all countries, life expectancy rises steadily with GDP per capita, and the box plot only needed to present that relationship cleanly. The AGAINST side was considerably more difficult. Our checkpoint chart on growth and longevity still showed a positive slope, and the feedback we received made it clear that a persuasive AGAINST visualization could not simply add caveats to a rising trend; it had to show a trend that does not rise. Achieving that required us to choose a subset of the data and a population filter that together flatten the line, and then to summarise the remaining countries with median bars that make the plateau visible at a glance. It is worth mentioning that the requirement to remove overplotting improved both charts: summarising into quintiles on one side and showing one point per country for a single year on the other made each message far easier to read.

What surprised us most was how few of our choices involved altering or fabricating data. Every number on both charts is correct, both charts cite the same World Bank source, and every filter is stated on the charts or on this page. Nevertheless, the two charts lead a reader to opposite conclusions, and they do so whilst sharing the same five income groups: the FOR chart compresses the richest fifth into one box, and the AGAINST chart enlarges that same box until its flatness fills the frame. The most powerful lever was a decision that looks like routine cleaning, dropping small countries for statistical stability, which the FOR chart does not do and the AGAINST chart does; the countries it removes are precisely the ones that contradict the AGAINST message. It is also worth acknowledging the limits of both arguments: “better health” is measured here by life expectancy alone, and the AGAINST chart tests the association among rich countries rather than whether wealth predicts health across the full range of countries.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization as work in which the reader could reach the same conclusion if they had access to the full data and the reasons behind every transformation. Under that definition, the bound between acceptable persuasion and misleading design lies less in any particular technique and more in whether the choice changes the answer. Emphasis, clear titles, annotations and a sensible axis range are acceptable when the omitted data would not overturn the message. A choice becomes misleading when it is selected because of its effect on the conclusion, and when its consequences are hidden even though the rule itself is disclosed, as with our population filter, which cuts the slope by more than a third and nearly halves the correlation whilst sounding purely methodological. We therefore believe that disclosure alone is not sufficient: an ethical chart should also make clear what the excluded data would have shown. The section below applies that test to our own work.

What Both Charts Leave Out

To put our own definition into practice, we drew one further chart that neither persuasive visualization shows: every country and territory on a single log-scale plot, with the countries used by the AGAINST chart filled in, the small members of the richest fifth that it drops drawn as hollow circles, and everything else in grey. It is collapsed by default so that each persuasive chart can first be judged on its own terms.

Show the full data behind both charts

Seen together, the two persuasive charts are both accurate about the part of the data they show. The FOR chart is the more faithful summary of the whole picture, because the relationship is strong across most of the income range; the AGAINST chart is accurate only for a slice of 25 countries that we selected, and the slice is partly defined by a filter that removes the countries most likely to contradict it.
