## Write-up

### Why we asked this question

Most people picture thunderstorms in the late afternoon, and we wanted to see whether that holds up when you look at a satellite that watches the whole of the Americas at once, and whether it is also true over the ocean. The Geostationary Lightning Mapper on GOES-16 records where and when each flash happens, which means we can ask "what hour?" for every one degree cell from Canada down to Argentina. One map of total lightning shows where storms are common but not when they happen, so we built the page around moving through the hours of the day.

### Data and how we transformed it

We used the GLM L2 LCFA product from NOAA's open data bucket on AWS (s3://noaa-goes16/GLM-L2-LCFA/). GLM writes a new file every 20 seconds, which is too many files to download for six years, so we took one in fifteen available files for every day from 2019 to 2024, 2,192 days in total. From each file we kept the latitude, the longitude and the quality flag of every flash, and dropped any flash that was not flagged as good. The flashes were then counted per one degree cell and per hour, once in UTC and once in local solar time (the UTC hour plus the longitude divided by 15), and averaged over the six years so the map shows a typical year of about 22.5 million sampled flashes. A one degree cell gets smaller the further it is from the equator, so the map colours flashes per 10,000 km2 rather than the raw count, whilst the tooltip still gives both numbers. We also removed one cell over the Pacific that stayed active at every hour of the day from late 2021 to 2024, as it is a GLM false-event source and not weather; in 2019 and 2020 that cell was quiet and we kept it.

The archive listings sum to 628,930 sampled files, compared with 631,296 nominal five-minute slots. Some days have fewer files and one has more, so taking every fifteenth file does not always mean exactly five minutes between samples. The original downloader also counted listed files when a download failed. The saved grids cannot recover how many downloads succeeded, so we do not claim complete coverage or adjust the counts for missing coverage. The corrected downloader rejects incomplete days on future runs.

To separate land and ocean, we tested a four by four set of points inside each cell against Natural Earth's land outline. A cell counts as land when at least half of those points are on land. We also count Lake Maracaibo's inland-water cell as land rather than open ocean. These are whole-cell groups, so coastal cells can still contain both land and water.

### Design rationale

The map draws one square per cell, where position shows the place and colour shows how much lightning falls there in the selected hour. For the colour we went with a single ramp from pale yellow to dark red (YlOrRd) on a square root scale. The densities go from zero to several thousand, and on a linear scale most of the map looked empty, while on a log scale a cell with three flashes looked almost as strong as one with three hundred. Since the ramp gets steadily darker it can still be read by people with red-green colour blindness and in greyscale. The scale stays the same for every hour and for all four seasons, so a darker night map is a real difference. The whole-year view gets its own scale and the legend redraws when you switch to it.

The hour slider and the Play button are the main control, as the pattern only appears when you move across hours. Over land the lightning builds through the afternoon, peaks at 4 PM and is at its lowest around 9 AM, but over the ocean it stays fairly high through the night, with about 42% of ocean flashes falling between 9 PM and 6 AM against about 26% over land. Part of that gap comes from GLM itself, since it picks up fewer flashes in daylight, so the curves compare shapes and not totals. The switch between local solar time and UTC is there because in UTC the afternoon peak just marches west with the sun, while in local time it happens over most land at about the same hour.

Hovering over a cell (or tapping it on a phone) shows its count, its density and its share of the day, and sketches that cell's curve as a dotted line in the chart before you click. Dragging across the line chart also moves the hour, so the chart works as a second slider. Clicking a cell draws its 24 hour curve in the chart beside the map, next to a solid grey curve for all land cells and a dashed grey one for all ocean cells, and a second click adds another cell to compare. Clicking a picked cell again removes it. The first pick is drawn in orange and the second in blue, on the map as well as in the chart, so it is clear which square belongs to which curve, and both stand out against the grey references. The jump buttons take you straight to three places with very different rhythms, Central Florida, the Great Plains and Lake Maracaibo, and set the slider to that cell's busiest hour. Across the wider Great Plains region (35 to 48 N, 94 to 104 W) about 45% of the lightning comes at night, and the single Nebraska cell that the button picks peaks around 11 PM over the whole year. Each curve is divided by its own busiest hour to compare shapes rather than raw totals. That scaling does not correct GLM's daylight or edge-of-view detection bias. The season buttons and the six year chart underneath, with one solid grey line for land and one dashed grey line for ocean per year (the same marks as the land and ocean curves in the chart above, which keeps orange and blue for the two picked cells), came once we had more than one summer of data, to check that the shape of the day is not just one year's weather. In that lower chart, each land line uses that year's land total and each ocean line uses that year's ocean total.

Before settling on this we tried other designs. In our exploratory graphs we made a static grid of the same map at 3 AM, 9 AM, 3 PM and 9 PM, and another one for the four seasons, and while they show the big picture it is hard to follow one place through the day across separate panels. We did not plot every flash as a dot, since we expected millions of points to merge Florida and Colombia into blobs, and we expected bubbles to be harder to read than colour on a grid this dense. An animation without a slider would not let the reader stop at 3 AM over the Great Plains. Land and ocean lines on their own show when lightning happens but not where, so we kept them only as the grey reference.

### Development process

Yash built most of the project, which includes the pipeline that downloads and counts the GLM files, the exploratory graphs and the D3 page, with the help of AI coding tools along the way. Chau wrote our original plan for the question and the data, recorded and submitted the checkpoint video, and reviewed the work. In total we spent about 20 people-hours on the project, most of them on Yash's side. The part that took the longest was getting the data out of S3. Our home connection only managed about half a megabyte per second and a single summer took around five hours to download, so for the six years we ran the same script as GitHub Actions jobs next to the bucket, which got through all 72 months in under ten minutes. Some smaller problems also took longer than we expected, such as deciding which cells count as land along coastlines like Lake Maracaibo, and getting the map to fit the GLM field of view in D3. What surprised us most was that the late afternoon peak over land came at the same hour in all six years.
