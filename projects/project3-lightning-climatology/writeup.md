## Write-up

### Why we asked this question

Most people picture thunderstorms in the late afternoon, and we wanted to see whether that holds up when you look at a satellite that watches the whole of the Americas at once, and whether it is also true over the ocean. The Geostationary Lightning Mapper on GOES-16 records where and when each flash happens, which means we can ask "what hour?" for every one degree cell from Canada down to Argentina. One map of total lightning shows where storms are common but not when they happen, so we built the page around moving through the hours of the day.

### Data and how we transformed it

We used the GLM L2 LCFA product from NOAA's open data bucket on AWS (s3://noaa-goes16/GLM-L2-LCFA/). GLM writes a new file every 20 seconds, which is too many files to download for six years, so we took one in fifteen available files for every day from 2019 to 2024, 2,192 days in total. From each file we kept the latitude, the longitude and the quality flag of every flash, and dropped any flash that was not flagged as good. The flashes were then counted per one degree cell and per hour, once in UTC and once in local solar time (the UTC hour plus the longitude divided by 15), and averaged over the six years so the map shows a typical year of about 22.5 million sampled flashes. A one degree cell gets smaller the further it is from the equator, so the map colours flashes per 10,000 km2 rather than the raw count, whilst the tooltip still gives both numbers. We also removed one cell over the Pacific that stayed active at every hour of the day from late 2021 to 2024, as it is a GLM false-event source and not weather; in 2019 and 2020 that cell was quiet and we kept it.

The archive listings sum to 628,930 sampled files, compared with 631,296 nominal five-minute slots. Some days have fewer files and one has more, so taking every fifteenth file does not always mean exactly five minutes between samples. The original downloader also counted listed files when a download failed. The saved grids cannot recover how many downloads succeeded, so we do not claim complete coverage or adjust the counts for missing coverage. The corrected downloader rejects incomplete days on future runs.

To separate land and ocean, we tested a four by four set of points inside each cell against Natural Earth's land outline. A cell counts as land when at least half of those points are on land. We also count Lake Maracaibo's inland-water cell as land rather than open ocean. These are whole-cell groups, so coastal cells can still contain both land and water.

### Design rationale

We went through a couple of versions of the plan, as we did not start off with six years of data. Our original plan was to use one week of July, and then we realized that ocean cells had too few flashes to show any real pattern. Therefore, we expanded from one week to one summer, then to a full year, and eventually ended up with six years.

For the map each one degree grid cell is a coloured square, where position shows the place and colour shows how much lightning falls there in the selected hour. For colour we used pale yellow to dark red (YlOrRd) on a square-root scale. The densities go from zero to several thousand, so a linear scale made most of the map look empty, and a log scale made tiny counts look as big as huge ones (a cell with three flashes looked almost as strong as one with three hundred). Since the ramp gets steadily darker it can still be read by people with red-green colour blindness and in greyscale. We kept the scale the same across every hour and all four seasons so night and winter actually look dimmer. The whole-year view gets its own scale and the legend redraws when you switch to it.

The main part of our page is an hour slider with a Play button, since our question is about when lightning happens and a single map can't actually show that. Over land the lightning builds through the afternoon, peaks at 4 PM and is at its lowest around 9 AM, but over the ocean it stays fairly high through the night, with about 42% of ocean flashes falling between 9 PM and 6 AM against about 26% over land. Part of that gap comes from GLM itself, since it picks up fewer flashes in daylight, so the curves compare shapes and not totals. We also added a local time / UTC toggle, because in UTC the afternoon peak just marches west with the sun, while in local time it happens over most land at about the same hour.

Hovering over a cell (or tapping it on a phone) shows its count, its density and its share of the day, and sketches that cell's curve as a dotted line in the chart before you click. Dragging across the line chart also moves the hour, so the chart works as a second slider. We added click-to-compare so you can see any cell's 24-hour curve: clicking a cell draws its curve in the chart beside the map, next to a solid grey curve for all land cells and a dashed grey one for all ocean cells, and a second click adds another cell to compare. Clicking a picked cell again removes it. The first pick is drawn in orange and the second in blue, on the map as well as in the chart, so it is clear which square belongs to which curve, and both stand out against the grey references. The jump buttons take you straight to three places with very different rhythms, Central Florida, the Great Plains and Lake Maracaibo, and set the slider to that cell's busiest hour. Across the wider Great Plains region (35 to 48 N, 94 to 104 W) about 45% of the lightning comes at night, and the single Nebraska cell that the button picks peaks around 11 PM over the whole year. Each curve is divided by its own busiest hour to compare shapes rather than raw totals. That scaling does not correct GLM's daylight or edge-of-view detection bias.

The season switch came once we had more than one summer of data. For the six-year version, we added a small chart underneath with one line per year for land (solid grey) and one per year for ocean (dashed grey). These are the same marks as the land and ocean curves in the chart above, which keeps orange and blue for the two picked cells. It shows the pattern held every year and is not just one year's weather. In that lower chart, each land line uses that year's land total and each ocean line uses that year's ocean total.

Before settling on this we tried other designs. We thought about using 24 small maps instead of a slider. In our exploratory graphs we made a static grid of the same map at 3 AM, 9 AM, 3 PM and 9 PM, and another one for the four seasons, and while they show the big picture it is hard to follow one place through the day across separate panels. We did not plot every flash as a dot, since we expected millions of flashes to turn the busy areas like Florida and Colombia into solid blobs, and we expected bubbles to overlap too much in the hotspots. An animation without a slider would not let the reader stop at 3 AM over the Great Plains. Land and ocean lines on their own show when lightning happens but not where, so we kept them only as the grey reference.

### Development process

For splitting up the work, we were met with tight timelines, so we figured it would be better to split it into two separate parts, which are planning and ideas and then building the page. Yash came up with the project idea and built most of the project, which includes the pipeline that downloads and counts the GLM files, the exploratory graphs and the D3 map and interactions, with the help of AI coding tools along the way. Chau started the write-up at the checkpoint, before the page was fully built, recorded and submitted the checkpoint video, reviewed the work and revised this write-up.

We spent about 20 people-hours on the project, mostly on Yash's side for development of the actual page. Getting the data out of S3 took the longest. Our home internet only managed about half a megabyte per second, so one summer took around five hours to download. For the six-year version we ran the same script as GitHub Actions jobs close to where the data is stored, which got through all 72 months in under ten minutes. Smaller problems also added to the time, like deciding which cells count as land along coastlines such as Lake Maracaibo and getting the map to fit the satellite's view in D3. What surprised us most was that the late-afternoon peak over land came at the same hour in all six years.
