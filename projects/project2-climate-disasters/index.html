Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Alpaslan Ozbas, aozbas@ucsd.edu

Bora Vanli, bvanli@ucsd.edu

Yash Goel, a2goel@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Climate-related disasters are becoming more frequent.

Dataset: the IMF Climate Change Indicators Dashboard, table "Climate-related Disasters Frequency", which counts the climate-related disasters recorded each year from 1980 to 2022 in the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT, maintained by CRED at UCLouvain; the IMF copy was accessed on 31 March 2023). We downloaded the CSV from the dashboard's data service on 22 October 2026 and cached it in a data/ folder that is not part of the published site; running make_charts.py downloads the same file again. The table is wide, with one column per year, so the first step in the script pivots it to one row per country, disaster type and year. Each country has a TOTAL row and up to six type rows (flood, storm, drought, extreme temperature, landslide and wildfire); the six type rows sum exactly to the totals.

Sample: the table covers 215 countries and territories and we keep all of them. The 215 include about two dozen territories and dependencies (for example Hong Kong, Guam, Bermuda and Tokelau) and five former states and territories that only have records for their years of existence (for example East and West Germany, the Soviet Union, Serbia and Montenegro, and the Netherlands Antilles). We treat each row as a reporting unit and refer to them as countries and territories. No row is dropped. We treat empty cells as years in which EM-DAT recorded no disaster: 5,176 of the 9,245 cells in the TOTAL rows are empty and we read every one of them as zero. EM-DAT records events by country, so an event that affects several countries is counted in each. The world totals therefore count country-events, not distinct events.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Five-year period means instead of annual counts (score: -1). We averaged the counts over five-year periods because single years were too noisy. This smoothed the single-year peak of 407 in 2005 and softened the weaker years from 2011 to 2018, so the series reads as a climb with one dip before a new high. We kept the 2010-14 dip visible, at 312 against 356, which is why the score is only moderate. Annual counts would have shown every peak, but the series rises until 2000 and then bounces around 340, so the long rise gets lost.
  • A final period of only three years (score: -1). We used every year the table gave us, so the last bar covers 2020 to 2022. Only three years sit behind it and its mean is 370. One of those years, 2021, has 391, the second-highest in the table. The bar is drawn at the same width as the five-year bars and the headline "three times" comes from it, but nothing tells the reader that a three-year mean jumps around more. Ending at 2019 with a full five-year bar of 322 would have looked fairer, but it would have cut the three most recent years and shrunk the headline ratio.
  • Stacked bars showing every disaster type, on an axis from zero (score: +1.5). Instead of going with a single total line, we stacked the six types in one muted ramp with floods at the bottom, and the axis starts at zero. The reader sees the total and what it is made of. In 1980-84 floods averaged 44 per year, and by 2020-22 they were at 200. Storms add much of the rest of the growth, droughts stay roughly flat, and landslides only grew from a low base. A single total line would have been simpler, but it would have hidden the split.
  • A fixed panel of the 82 countries with records in every period (score: -1). We added a fixed panel of 82 countries and territories because the rise might come from countries that only started showing up in EM-DAT later. Each one has at least one record in every period, and together they hold about 80% of all records. In 1980-84 they averaged 104 per year, and by 2020-22 it was 297. It is still a picked group, and a country can report more events over time without suffering more of them. Showing only the left panel would have been cleaner, but then nobody could answer the coverage objection.
  • Title: "Recorded climate-related disasters are three times as frequent as in the early 1980s" (score: -0.5). We checked the title against both ratios. For all countries it is 370 against 122 per year, which works out to 3.0 times, and for the 82 countries with records in every period it is 297 against 104, or 2.8 times. The wording is calm and "recorded" keeps it to what the table counts. But "even in the same 82 countries" makes the rise sound like it cannot be a reporting effect, when those 82 were picked because they had records and their 2.8 rounds up to "three times". The title also does not say that a three-year mean is compared with a five-year mean, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. "More often recorded" would have been more careful, but it states the claim more weakly than the proposition.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Both panels start in 2000, not 1980 (score: -1.5). We started both panels in 2000 because EM-DAT coverage is better after that year, but the chart never makes this argument. The table begins in 1980, when 104 disasters were recorded, and by 2000 the count was 370. The right panel compares the 2000s with the 2010s and leaves out 2020 to 2022, which have the highest period mean in the table at 370 per year, so a reader sees twenty-three years of ups and downs and none of the twenty years of growth before them. Starting in 1980 would have been fairer, but the early rise would have dominated the chart and hurt our title. With equal weighting we think this is the most deceptive choice on this side.
  • A fitted trend line read as the story (score: -0.5). We drew a dashed least-squares line over the 23 annual counts. Its slope is -0.6 per year, which supports "held steady", but a line makes the plateau look more exact than it is. The counts range from 287 to 407, the last four years (2019 to 2022) all sit above the line, and a fit from 2014 onwards would slope upwards. We drew the raw points next to it, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. A moving average would have avoided a single slope, but its shape depends on the window length.
  • Axis from zero to 500 on the annual panel (score: +1). We started the value axis at zero and ran it to 500, so the year-to-year swings show at their real size next to the level. A truncated axis from 280 to 410 would have made the same series look like a collapse and then a rebound, but it would have exaggerated the swings. We did not give it +2 because the top of the axis is 500 and the data only need 450, so the line looks a bit flatter than it should.
  • Every country counted once in the decade comparison (score: -1.5). We binned countries by the change in their own decade total, so Romania's fall of 33 and a small island's fall of one each count as one country, and both sit inside the "107 recorded fewer". Of those 107, 57 fell by only one to four, and another 29 had five or fewer disasters in the 2000s. In the 2010s the ten countries with the most records account for 37% of all disasters. Equal weighting does answer how widespread the change was, and the world total also fell, from 3,483 to 3,171, but the headline counts are dominated by countries with very few events, and nothing on the chart says so. Plotting only the change in the world total would have been simpler, but it would have hidden how many countries moved each way.
  • Title: "Since 2000, the number of climate-related disasters recorded each year has held steady" (score: -0.5). We kept the wording calm, and the claim is true of the 23 years shown. Both titles say "recorded", so neither claims more than the table counts. "Since 2000" is honest about the window, but it quietly treats 2000 as a natural starting point. A reader who does not know the earlier years has no reason to ask what came before, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. "For more than two decades" would have sounded stronger, but it makes the window sound like all the history there is.

Final Reflection

Initially, we were not sure we could argue both sides of one topic. Our checkpoint used a different proposition (women's education and work), and for the final we picked climate disasters. The hardest part was choosing what to show, because the supporting chart was easy and the opposing chart was not. We had 1,333 recorded disasters in the 1980s, which went up to 2,215 in the 1990s and 3,483 in the 2000s. After that, the 2010s came in at 3,171, below the 2000s. The only opposing version that persuaded was the one that left out the twenty years before 2000. What surprised us was how much of the debate is about reporting and how little is about weather. The number of countries with any record in a year roughly doubled between the 1980s and the 2000s, so more records can come from more countries reporting.

To us, ethical visualization means letting the reader use the chart and its notes to see how a different reasonable analysis would reach a different conclusion. None of our ten decisions needed a truncated axis or an altered number. The problems sat in the shortened window and in the panel that looks like it closes the coverage question. Those choices change what gets counted, so a reader who only sees the image never meets them.

The line is where a choice only works if the reader does not know the alternative. A window or a unit of counting becomes misleading when we picked it because the other option weakens our conclusion. It is worth mentioning that the indicator counts events and not severity, whilst better reporting cannot be separated from any change in the climate, so the boundary is not sharp. Our test was whether we would be comfortable showing the reader the version we left out, and that version is the reference figure below. What we valued most was learning that a chart can be accurate in every number and still mislead.
