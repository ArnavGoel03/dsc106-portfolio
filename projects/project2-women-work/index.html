Alpaslan Ozbas, aozbas@ucsd.edu

Bora Vanli, bvanli@ucsd.edu

Yash Goel, a2goel@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Women's gains in education have not translated into gains in work.

Dataset: the course's Human Development Indicators, 1960-2020 dataset, from the World Bank World Development Indicators. We fetched 1990 to 2019 from the World Bank Indicators API on 21 October 2026 (Pacific time) into a cache, which make_charts.py reuses and refills only when it is missing. The indicators are SE.TER.ENRR.FE (female gross tertiary enrollment), SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS (female labor force participation, ages 15 and over) and SL.TLF.CACT.FM.ZS (female to male participation ratio).

Sample: the world lines use the World Bank's own world aggregate. The per-country panels keep the 98 countries and territories that report all three indicators in some year from 1990 to 2000 and in some year from 2014 to 2019. Of the 187 economies with participation data, the other 89 lack at least one indicator in one of the two windows and are dropped.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Enrollment and participation drawn on one percentage axis (score: -1). Both world series sit on one 0% to 60% scale in the left panel. The rising blue line and the flat brown line are easy to compare that way. But enrollment is measured against the university-age cohort and can go over 100%, while participation covers all women aged 15 and over, so the levels are not directly comparable. The opposing chart puts its two measures in separate panels with their own titles, and we scored it +1.5. Separate panels would have removed the false comparison of levels, but the gap would have been much harder to see, and the gap is the point of the chart. So we kept the shared axis.
  • Women's own participation rate instead of the female-to-male ratio (score: -1.5). We showed women's own participation rate and never the ratio of the female rate to the male rate. The world ratio rose from 66.9 to 68.2 because men's participation fell faster than women's, so on the relative measure women did gain ground. A reader cannot see an option that is missing, so we scored this as clearly deceptive, the same as the matching choice in the opposing chart. The rate also counts all women aged 15 and over, so rising enrollment can pull it down by itself, and between 1990 and 2019 the world rate for ages 15 to 24 fell from 50% to 34% while ages 15 to 64 only moved from 56% to 54%. The ratio would have been fairer for the gap, but it shows the world gaining, which weakens the point of the chart, so we kept women's own rate.
  • Paired histograms of per-country change on one shared axis (score: +1). For each of the 98 countries and territories we worked out the change in enrollment and in participation, in percentage points. We binned both into 5-point intervals and stacked the two histograms on one x-axis, with zero and the median marked. Enrollment stretches far to the right while participation stays piled up near zero. We scored this as earnest, since every retained country is included and we did not pick examples. A scatter plot of the two changes would have shown how each country moved on both measures at once, but with 98 overlapping points the difference in spread is hard to see quickly.
  • Median callouts in place of a fuller summary (score: -1). The two histograms only carry the callouts "Median country: +21 points" and "Median country: +1 point". Side by side, they push the reader to see the second as stagnation, but the picture is more mixed. Of the 98 countries, 58 raised women's participation, some by as much as 17.2 points, and the other 40 saw it fall. Both medians are accurate, but nothing on the chart says a majority gained, and those counts only appear in this write-up. Printing the count of gaining countries on each panel would have told people right away that most countries gained, but it would have softened the contrast between 21 and 1, so the counts stay in this write-up only.
  • Title framed on the world aggregate (score: -0.5). The title says "Worldwide, women's university enrollment tripled while their labor force participation slipped". That is accurate for the world lines it opens with, and the language is calm, but the slant comes from building the whole figure around the world totals. Enrollment goes from 13% to 41% and participation goes from 51% to 48%, while the median country in the right-hand panel gained just one point. We scored this as only mildly deceptive, since the statement is true and the median callout shows that one point gain. A title naming the median country change would have fit the right-hand panel better, but it would have been a flatter sentence that does not walk the reader through the world lines first, so the title stays on the world totals.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Opening with the largest-rise group (score: -1). The left panels show only the 25 countries and territories where enrollment rose the most. Median enrollment goes from 20.5% to 71.7%, a jump of +51.2 points, and the median participation ratio goes from 54.5% to 70.4%, which is +15.9 points. The pattern is strongest in this group, so putting it first makes the step from education to work look direct, and someone who stops at the left panels sees the best case and thinks it is typical. Opening with all 98 countries and territories would have been more representative, but the pattern is weaker there. So we led with the strongest group and named it in the caption.
  • Two small panels on one 0 to 80% scale (score: +1.5). Enrollment and the participation ratio are different measures, so each gets its own panel with its own title. Both share a 0 to 80% scale that starts at zero, which lets a reader compare the size of the two changes. The separate titles make clear that the levels are not comparable. We scored this as earnest, and it is the main difference from the single overlaid axis in the supporting chart, which got a -1. Two lines crossing on one plot would have been more compact and shown the timing of both changes together, but it invites a comparison of levels that means nothing for two different measures.
  • The female-to-male participation ratio instead of women's own rate (score: -1.5). We measured work with the ratio of the female participation rate to the male one, which rose in 65 of the 98 countries. We never show women's own participation rate. The ratio is a fair measure of the gender gap and the caption defines it, but it can rise just because men's participation falls. The world female rate declined from 51% to 48% over the same years, and hiding that is the same omission as in the supporting chart, so it gets the same score. Instead of going with a fuller picture that showed women's own rate next to the ratio, we showed only the ratio, because the two measures disagree and that makes the conclusion messier, which is more about persuasion than accuracy.
  • Group medians with middle-half bands (score: -1.5). We split the 98 countries into four equal groups by enrollment gain, with one dot for each group's median change in the ratio and a band over its middle half. The medians are +1.3, +3.4, +4.3 and +8.8 points, and the bands look rigorous, but they hide the tails, since individual changes run from -27.3 to +33.6 points. Across all 98 the correlation between the two changes is only 0.18 by Pearson and 0.24 by Spearman, and those correlations appear only in this write-up, as does the share of each group whose ratio rose (60%, 54%, 71% and 80%). The second group did worse than the first, the top group is the only one whose middle half sits fully above zero (+3.5 at its lower quartile), and it covers far more of the enrollment range than the other three, which the chart does not mark. We scored this as clearly deceptive, since a scatter of all 98 countries would have shown the weak 0.18 correlation, but it would also have wrecked the clean upward pattern, so we went with group medians.
  • Title framed as an association (score: -0.5). The title is "Where women's enrollment rose more, the gender gap in labor force participation narrowed more". It is true of the group medians and it avoids causal verbs, but a reader may infer that education caused the narrowing, and cross-country data cannot show that. Also, "the gap narrowed" does not say whether women rose or men fell. We scored this as mildly deceptive, since the wording is literally accurate and the subtitle says the comparison is between medians. A title saying only that the medians differ by group would have been more cautious and harder to read as causal, but it would be flatter and sound less like a headline, so we kept the association wording.

Final Reflection

Initially, the supporting side felt easier. Women's university enrollment roughly tripled whilst their labor force participation slipped from 51% to 48%. The hardest part was choosing what to show, because the opposing side needed a fair way to measure work. At the checkpoint we had used India, a chart by region and a chart by age group. The TA feedback asked for global data and enrollment on both sides. After that we rebuilt both charts on the same 98 countries and territories. We also tried indexing both rates to 1990 and plotting how much of the gap to equal participation had closed, but those versions were harder to read. What surprised us most was that the measure of work decides the conclusion. Women's own rate fell while the ratio of women to men rose.

To us, ethical visualization means making clear what the numbers can and cannot show. A visualization is ethical when a reader could tell how a different reasonable analysis would have reached another conclusion. It is worth mentioning that most of our disclosures sit beside the charts and hardly any are on them, so a reader of the image alone never sees them. Leaving the ratio out of the first chart does not meet our standard, and neither does leaving women's own rate out of the second.

The line is crossed when a choice only works if the reader does not know the alternative. None of our deceptive choices needed a truncated axis or a false number, but the median callouts, the best- case group and the group medians that hide a correlation of 0.18 all landed on the wrong side. That stays misleading even when every number is correct. What we learned is that a chart can be accurate in every number and still mislead, and we now value the honest version of an argument more than the persuasive one, even when it is messier.
