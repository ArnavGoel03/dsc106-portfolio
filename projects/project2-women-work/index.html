Project 2 Persuasive or Deceptive Visualization?

Alpaslan Ozbas, aozbas@ucsd.edu

Bora Vanli, bvanli@ucsd.edu

Yash Goel, a2goel@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Women's gains in education have not translated into gains in work.

Dataset: the course's Human Development Indicators, 1960-2020 dataset, from the World Bank World Development Indicators. We fetched 1990 to 2019 from the World Bank Indicators API on 21 October 2026 (Pacific time) into a cache, which make_charts.py reuses and refills only when it is missing. The indicators are SE.TER.ENRR.FE (female gross tertiary enrollment), SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS (female labor force participation, ages 15 and over) and SL.TLF.CACT.FM.ZS (female to male participation ratio).

Sample: the world lines use the World Bank's own world aggregate. The per-country panels keep the 98 countries and territories that report all three indicators in some year from 1990 to 2000 and in some year from 2014 to 2019. Of the 187 economies with participation data, the other 89 lack at least one indicator in one of the two windows and are dropped.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Enrollment and participation drawn on one percentage axis (score: -1). Both world series sit on one 0% to 60% scale in the left panel. The rising blue line and the flat brown line are easy to compare that way. Honestly, that is the problem. Enrollment is measured against the university-age cohort and can go over 100%. Participation is measured against all women aged 15 and over. So the gap looks like one line failing to keep up with the other. The opposing chart puts its two measures in separate panels with their own titles, and we scored it +1.5. We also thought about splitting ours the same way. Separate panels would have removed the false comparison of levels, but the gap would be much harder to see, and the gap is the whole point of the chart. So we kept the shared axis.
  • Women's own participation rate instead of the female-to-male ratio (score: -1.5). We show women's own participation rate and never the ratio of the female rate to the male rate. That was a big call. The world ratio actually rose from 66.9 to 68.2, because men's participation fell faster than women's. So on the relative measure, women did gain ground. A reader cannot see an option that is missing, so we scored this as clearly deceptive, the same as the matching choice in the opposing chart. The rate also has a catch. It counts all women aged 15 and over, so a woman in full-time study counts as outside the labor force. Rising enrollment can pull the rate down by itself. Take 1990 and 2019. For ages 15 to 24 the world rate went from 50% down to 34%. For ages 15 to 64 it only moved from 56% to 54%. The ratio would have been fairer for the gap. It also shows the world gaining, which kills the point of the chart, so we kept women's own rate.
  • Paired histograms of per-country change on one shared axis (score: +1). For each of the 98 countries and territories we worked out how much enrollment and participation changed, in percentage points. Then we binned both into 5-point intervals and stacked the two histograms on one x-axis, with zero and the median marked. Enrollment stretches far to the right. Participation stays piled up near zero. That contrast is what persuades. We scored this as earnest, since every retained country is in there and we did not pick examples. We also thought about one scatter plot of the two changes. It would show how each country moved on both measures at once. With 98 overlapping points though, the difference in spread is hard to see quickly, so we kept the histograms.
  • Median callouts in place of a fuller summary (score: -1). The two histograms only carry the callouts "Median country: +21 points" and "Median country: +1 point". Side by side, they push the reader to see the second one as stagnation. It is more mixed than that. Of the 98 countries, 58 raised women's participation. Some rose by as much as 17.2 points. The other 40 saw it fall. Both medians are accurate. Still, nothing on the chart says a majority gained, and those counts only appear in this write-up. We could have printed the count of gaining countries on each panel. That would have told people right away that most countries gained. It would also have softened the contrast between 21 and 1, so the counts stay in this write-up only.
  • Title framed on the world aggregate (score: -0.5). The title says "Worldwide, women's university enrollment tripled while their labor force participation slipped". That is accurate for the world lines it opens with, and the language is calm. The slant comes from building the whole figure around the world totals. Enrollment goes from 13% to 41%. Participation goes from 51% to 48%. The median country in the right-hand panel gained just one point. We scored this as only mildly deceptive. The statement is true and the median callout shows that one point gain. A title naming the median country change would fit the right-hand panel better. It would also be a flatter sentence that does not walk the reader through the world lines first. We did not want that, so the title stays on the world totals.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Opening with the largest-rise group (score: -1). The left panels show only the 25 countries and territories where enrollment rose the most. Median enrollment goes from 20.5% to 71.7%, a jump of +51.2 points. The median participation ratio goes from 54.5% to 70.4%, which is +15.9 points. The pattern is strongest in this group, so putting it first makes the step from education to work look direct. Someone who stops at the left panels sees the best case and thinks it is typical. At first we wanted to open with all 98 countries and territories. That would be more representative, but the pattern is weaker there. So we led with the strongest group and named it in the caption.
  • Two small panels on one 0 to 80% scale (score: +1.5). Enrollment and the participation ratio are different measures, so each gets its own panel with its own title. Both share a 0 to 80% scale that starts at zero. That lets a reader compare the size of the two changes. The separate titles make clear that the levels are not comparable. We see this as earnest, and it is the main difference from the single overlaid axis in the supporting chart, which got a -1. We did think about two lines crossing on one plot. It is more compact and shows the timing of both changes together. We dropped it because it invites a comparison of levels, and that means nothing for two different measures.
  • The female-to-male participation ratio instead of women's own rate (score: -1.5). We measure work with the ratio of the female participation rate to the male one. It rose in 65 of the 98 countries. We never show women's own participation rate. The ratio is a fair measure of the gender gap and the caption defines it. Still, it can rise just because men's participation falls. The world female rate did decline from 51% to 48% over the same years. Hiding that is the same omission as in the supporting chart, so it gets the same score. Showing women's own rate next to the ratio would have been a fuller and more honest picture of work. The two measures disagree, though, which makes the conclusion messier. Showing only the ratio keeps things tidy. Honestly, that reason is more about persuasion than accuracy.
  • Group medians with middle-half bands (score: -1.5). We split the 98 countries into four equal groups by enrollment gain. Each group gets one dot for its median change in the ratio, with a band over its middle half. The first two medians are +1.3 and +3.4 points. The last two are +4.3 and +8.8. The bands look rigorous. They also hide the tails, since individual changes run from -27.3 to +33.6 points. Across all 98 the correlation between the two changes is only 0.18 by Pearson. By Spearman it is 0.24. Those correlations appear only in this write-up. So does the share of each group whose ratio rose. The first two groups are at 60% and 54%, and the last two are at 71% and 80%. The second group did worse than the first. The top group is the only one whose middle half sits fully above zero, at +3.5 for its lower quartile. It also covers far more of the enrollment range than the other three, and the chart marks neither. We scored this as clearly deceptive. A scatter of all 98 countries would have shown the weak 0.18 correlation. It would also have wrecked the clean upward pattern, so we went with group medians.
  • Title framed as an association (score: -0.5). The title is "Where women's enrollment rose more, the gender gap in labor force participation narrowed more". It is true of the group medians and it avoids causal verbs. Still, a reader may infer that education caused the narrowing, and cross-country data cannot show that. Also, "the gap narrowed" does not say whether women rose or men fell. We scored this as mildly deceptive. The wording is literally accurate and the subtitle says the comparison is between medians. A title saying only that the medians differ by group would be more cautious and harder to read as causal. It is also flatter and sounds less like a headline, so we kept the association wording.

Final Reflection

Initially, the supporting side felt easier. Women's university enrollment roughly tripled whilst their labor force participation slipped from 51% to 48%. The opposing side was harder. At the checkpoint we had used India, a chart by region and a chart by age group. The TA feedback asked for global data, enrollment on both sides and a clearer opposing chart. After that we rebuilt both charts on the same 98 countries and territories. What surprised us most was how much the conclusion depends on the measure of work. Women's own rate fell and the ratio of women to men rose. So each side could just pick the number that suited it.

We now think a visualization is ethical when a reader could tell how a different reasonable analysis would have reached another conclusion. By that standard, the disclosures in this write-up are some of the better parts of our project. It is worth mentioning that most disclosures sit beside the charts. Hardly any are on them, so a reader of the image alone never sees them. Leaving the ratio out of the first chart does not meet our standard. Neither does leaving women's own rate out of the second. Both charts also count gains in work as joining the labor force, which says nothing about wages or hours.

For us, acceptable persuasion points the reader at something that is really in the data. None of our deceptive choices needed a truncated axis or a false number. Every percentage axis starts at zero and every figure cites its source. The line is crossed when a choice only works if the reader does not know the alternative. Instead of going with the option that weakens the case, we picked the one that helps it. That stays misleading even when every number is correct. The median callouts, the best-case group and the group medians that hide a correlation of 0.18 all landed on the wrong side. Our test is simple. Would we be comfortable showing the reader the version we threw away?
