Proposition: Women's gains in education have not translated into gains in work.

Dataset: the course's Human Development Indicators, 1960-2020 dataset, taken from its source, the World Bank World Development Indicators. We fetched the years 1990 to 2019 from the World Bank Indicators API on 21 October 2026 (Pacific time) and cached them in a data/ folder, which is not part of the published site. Running make_charts.py fetches the same indicators again. The indicators are female gross tertiary enrollment (SE.TER.ENRR.FE), female labor force participation, ages 15 and over (SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS), and the ratio of the female to the male labor force participation rate (SL.TLF.CACT.FM.ZS).

Sample: the world lines use the World Bank's own world aggregate. The per-country panels in both figures keep only the economies that report all three indicators in some year from 1990 to 2000 and again in some year from 2014 to 2019, and use each one's earliest and latest such years. Of the 187 countries and territories with participation data, 98 pass this test; the other 89 lack enrollment data in one of the two windows and are dropped, including Brazil, Japan, Germany, France, Italy, Nigeria and Australia. The dropped economies are disproportionately high-income (46 of the 89, against 17 of the 98 retained), so the sample leans towards middle-income countries. The 98 include one territory (West Bank and Gaza), so we refer to them as countries and territories.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Enrollment and participation drawn on one percentage axis (score: -1). The left panel places both world series on a single 0% to 60% scale, which invites the reader to compare the rising blue line with the flat brown line directly. That is deceptive here because the two measures have different denominators: gross tertiary enrollment is measured against the university-age cohort (and can exceed 100%), whilst participation is measured against all women aged 15 and over. Two different measures on one scale invite a comparison of levels, and the gap then looks like one series failing to follow the other. The separate panels in the opposing chart (+1.5) are the earnest counterpart of this choice, so the two scores are consistent. Alternative considered: two separate panels, which would be more honest but far less persuasive.
  • Women's own participation rate instead of the female-to-male ratio (score: -1.5). We measure work with the female participation rate and never show the ratio of the female to the male rate. Over the same period the world ratio rose from 66.9 to 68.2, because men's participation fell faster than women's, so by the relative measure women did gain ground. A reader cannot notice the absence of the alternative, so we score this as clearly deceptive. It mirrors the measure choice in the opposing chart and receives the same score. Alternative considered: plotting the ratio, which we rejected because it weakens the case that work did not improve.
  • Paired histograms of per-country change on one shared axis (score: +1). For each of the 98 countries and territories we compute the percentage-point change in enrollment and in participation, bin both into 5-point intervals, and stack the two histograms over the same x-axis, with zero and the median marked. The persuasion comes from the contrast in spread: the enrollment distribution stretches far to the right whilst the participation distribution stays piled around zero. We score this as earnest, because it shows every retained country rather than chosen examples. Alternative considered: one scatter plot of the two changes, which we rejected because it shows the difference in spread less directly.
  • Median callouts in place of a fuller summary (score: -1). The two histograms carry only "Median country: +21 points" and "Median country: +1 point", which invites the reader to set 21 against 1 and read the second as stagnation. In fact 58 of the 98 raised women's participation, by as much as 17.2 points, whilst 40 saw it fall. Both medians are accurate, but nothing on the chart shows that a majority of countries gained, and these counts appear only in this write-up. Alternative considered: printing the count of gaining countries on each panel, which we rejected because it softens the contrast.
  • Title framed on the world aggregate (score: -0.5). The title, "Worldwide, women's university enrollment tripled; their labor force participation did not rise", is accurate for the world lines it opens with (13% to 41%, and 51% to 48%) and uses calm language. It nevertheless frames the whole figure around the world totals, whereas the median country in the right-hand panel gained one point. We score this as only mildly deceptive, since the statement is true and the median callout shows that one-point gain. Alternative considered: a title naming the median country change, which we rejected because it is less striking.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Opening with the largest-rise group (score: -1). The left panels show only the 25 countries and territories whose enrollment rose most. Their median enrollment rises from 20.5% to 71.7% (+51.2 points) and the median participation ratio from 54.5% to 70.4% (+15.9 points). The pattern is strongest in this group, so showing it first makes the step from education to work look direct. The group is named in the heading, but a reader who stops at the left panels sees the best case as though it were typical. Alternative considered: opening with all 98 countries, which we rejected because the pattern is weaker.
  • Two small panels on one 0 to 80% scale (score: +1.5). Enrollment and the participation ratio are different measures, so we draw them in separate, separately titled panels. Both share a 0 to 80% scale starting at zero, so the steeper education slope is shown at its true size relative to the work slope. We regard this as earnest, and it is the counterpart of the single shared axis in the supporting chart (-1). Alternative considered: two crossing lines on one plot, which we rejected because they invite a comparison of levels that means nothing.
  • The female-to-male participation ratio instead of women's own rate (score: -1.5). We measure work with the ratio of the female to the male participation rate, which rose in 65 of the 98, and never show women's own participation rate. The ratio is a defensible measure of the gender gap and is defined in the caption. It can rise simply because men's participation falls, however, and the world female rate declined from 51% to 48% over the same years. Hiding the alternative is the same omission as in the supporting chart, so it receives the same score. Alternative considered: showing women's own rate beside the ratio, which we rejected because it weakens the conclusion.
  • Group medians with middle-half bands (score: -1.5). We split the 98 into four equal groups by enrollment gain and plot each group's median change in the ratio (+1.3, +3.4, +4.3 and +8.8 points) as a dot, with a band covering its middle half. The bands look rigorous, yet they hide the tails (individual changes run from -27.3 to +33.6 points). Across all 98 the relationship is weak: the correlation between the two changes is 0.18 (Pearson) and 0.24 (Spearman). These figures appear only in this write-up, as does the share of each group whose ratio rose (60%, 54%, 71% and 80%), so a smaller share of the second group rose than of the first (54% against 60%). The only callout marks the lower quartile of the top group (+3.5 points), the one band wholly above zero, and the top group spans far more of the enrollment range than the other three. Because the chart announces none of this, we score it as clearly deceptive. Alternative considered: a scatter of all 98 countries, which we rejected because it would show the weak correlation.
  • Title framed as an association (score: -0.5). The title, "Where women's enrollment rose more, the gender gap in labor force participation narrowed more", is true of the group medians and avoids causal verbs. It still invites the reader to infer that education caused the narrowing, which cross-country data cannot show, and "the gap narrowed" does not say whether women rose or men fell. We score this as mildly deceptive, since the wording is literally accurate and the subtitle states that the comparison is between medians. Alternative considered: a title stating only that the medians differ by group, which we rejected because it is less persuasive.

Final Reflection

The supporting chart was the more straightforward to build, because the world figures alone make the case: enrollment roughly tripled whilst participation fell. The opposing side was harder. The TA feedback observed that our checkpoint did not show the enrollment trend or clearly oppose the proposition, and it asked that the supporting side use global rather than single-country data. Meeting both requests put the two charts on the same 98 countries and territories, which made an honest opposing case harder. The most surprising finding was that the choice of work measure decides the conclusion. Women's own participation rate fell worldwide, whereas the ratio of the female to the male rate rose, so each side could pick the indicator that suited it without altering a single value.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization as work in which the reader could reconstruct, from the chart and its notes, the main ways a different reasonable analysis would have reached a different conclusion. By that standard, the middle-half bands and the disclosures in this write-up are ethical additions. Keeping those disclosures beside the charts rather than on them is a weakness, since a reader who sees only the image never meets them. Omitting the ratio from the first chart and women's own rate from the second is not ethical by this standard. Both charts also measure "gains in work" only as attachment to the labor market, which says nothing about wages, hours or informal work.

Acceptable persuasion directs attention to something genuinely in the data. Emphasis, ordering and a clear headline qualify. None of our deceptive decisions needed a truncated axis or a false number: every axis includes zero and every figure cites its source. The line is crossed when a choice depends on the reader not knowing an alternative. A measure, threshold or aggregation chosen because the obvious alternative weakens the conclusion is misleading even when every number is correct. Median callouts that make modest gains read as stagnation, a best-case group shown first, and group medians that turn a correlation of 0.18 into a clean upward sequence all fall on the wrong side. A practical test is whether the designer would be comfortable showing the reader the discarded version.
