Proposition: Women's gains in education have not translated into gains in work.

Dataset: the course's Human Development Indicators, 1960-2020 dataset, from the World Bank World Development Indicators. We fetched 1990 to 2019 from the World Bank Indicators API on 21 October 2026 (Pacific time) into a cache, which make_charts.py reuses and refills only when it is missing. The indicators are SE.TER.ENRR.FE (female gross tertiary enrollment), SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS (female labor force participation, ages 15 and over) and SL.TLF.CACT.FM.ZS (female to male participation ratio).

Sample: the world lines use the World Bank's own world aggregate. The per-country panels keep the 98 countries and territories that report all three indicators in some year from 1990 to 2000 and in some year from 2014 to 2019. The other 89 lack data in one window and are dropped.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Enrollment and participation drawn on one percentage axis (score: -1). The left panel places both world series on a single 0% to 60% scale, which invites the reader to compare the rising blue line with the flat brown line directly. That is deceptive here because the two measures have different denominators: gross tertiary enrollment is measured against the university-age cohort (and can exceed 100%), whilst participation is measured against all women aged 15 and over. Two different measures on one scale invite a comparison of levels, and the gap then looks like one series failing to follow the other. The opposing chart draws its two measures in separate, separately titled panels and scores +1.5. A pair of separate panels would have removed the false comparison of levels, but the gap between the two lines then reads far less directly, and that gap is what carries the claim, so we kept the shared axis.
  • Women's own participation rate instead of the female-to-male ratio (score: -1.5). We measure work with the female participation rate and never show the ratio of the female to the male rate. Over the same period the world ratio rose from 66.9 to 68.2, because men's participation fell faster than women's, so by the relative measure women did gain ground. A reader cannot notice the absence of the alternative, so we score this as clearly deceptive, the same as the matching choice in the opposing chart. Plotting the female-to-male ratio would have been the fairer measure of the gender gap, since it captures men's participation falling too, but it shows the world gaining, which undoes the point of the chart, so we kept the women's own rate.
  • Paired histograms of per-country change on one shared axis (score: +1). For each of the 98 countries and territories we compute the percentage-point change in enrollment and in participation, bin both into 5-point intervals, and stack the two histograms over the same x-axis, with zero and the median marked. The persuasion comes from the contrast in spread: the enrollment distribution stretches far to the right whilst the participation distribution stays piled around zero. We score this as earnest, because it shows every retained country rather than chosen examples. A single scatter plot of the two changes would have shown how each country moved on both measures at once. With 98 overlapping points, however, the difference in spread is harder to see at a glance, so we kept the stacked histograms.
  • Median callouts in place of a fuller summary (score: -1). The two histograms carry only "Median country: +21 points" and "Median country: +1 point", which invites the reader to set 21 against 1 and read the second as stagnation. In fact 58 of the 98 raised women's participation, by as much as 17.2 points, whilst 40 saw it fall. Both medians are accurate, but nothing on the chart shows that a majority of countries gained, and these counts appear only in this write-up. Printing the count of gaining countries on each panel would have told the reader at once that most countries gained. It would also have blunted the contrast between 21 and 1, so we left the counts for this write-up.
  • Title framed on the world aggregate (score: -0.5). The title, "Worldwide, women's university enrollment tripled while their labor force participation slipped", is accurate for the world lines it opens with (13% to 41%, and 51% to 48%) and uses calm language. The slant comes only from framing the whole figure on the world totals, whereas the median country in the right-hand panel gained one point. We score this as only mildly deceptive, since the statement is true and the median callout shows that one-point gain. A title naming the median country change would have been more representative of the right-hand panel. It is a flatter sentence and would not have led the reader through the world lines first, so we kept the world framing.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Opening with the largest-rise group (score: -1). The left panels show only the 25 countries and territories whose enrollment rose most. Their median enrollment rises from 20.5% to 71.7% (+51.2 points) and the median participation ratio from 54.5% to 70.4% (+15.9 points). The pattern is strongest in this group, so showing it first makes the step from education to work look direct. A reader who stops at the left panels sees the best case as though it were typical. Opening with all 98 countries and territories would have been more representative and would have spared the reader a best case, but the pattern is weaker there, so we opened with the strongest group and named it in the caption.
  • Two small panels on one 0 to 80% scale (score: +1.5). Enrollment and the participation ratio are different measures, so we draw them in separate, separately titled panels. Both share a 0 to 80% scale starting at zero, so the two changes can be compared in size, whilst the separate titles make clear that the levels are not comparable. This is what distinguishes it from the single overlaid axis in the supporting chart (-1), so we regard it as earnest. Two lines crossing on one plot would have been compact and would have shown the timing of both changes together. They also invite a comparison of levels that means nothing for two different measures, so we chose separate panels.
  • The female-to-male participation ratio instead of women's own rate (score: -1.5). We measure work with the ratio of the female to the male participation rate, which rose in 65 of the 98, and never show women's own participation rate. It is a defensible measure of the gender gap and is defined in the caption, but it can rise simply because men's participation falls, and the world female rate declined from 51% to 48% over the same years. Hiding the alternative is the same omission as in the supporting chart, so it receives the same score. Showing women's own rate beside the ratio would have given a fuller and more honest account of work. The two measures disagree, which complicates the conclusion, so we kept only the ratio.
  • Group medians with middle-half bands (score: -1.5). We split the 98 into four equal groups by enrollment gain and plot each group's median change in the ratio (+1.3, +3.4, +4.3 and +8.8 points) as a dot, with a band covering its middle half. The bands look rigorous, yet they hide the tails (individual changes run from -27.3 to +33.6 points), and across all 98 the correlation between the two changes is only 0.18 (Pearson) and 0.24 (Spearman), figures that appear only in this write-up, as does the share of each group whose ratio rose (60%, 54%, 71% and 80%), so a smaller share of the second group rose than of the first (54% against 60%). The top group is the only one whose middle half lies wholly above zero (+3.5 at its lower quartile), which the chart does not mark and this write-up notes, and it spans far more of the enrollment range than the other three. We score it as clearly deceptive because the chart announces none of this. A scatter of all 98 countries would have shown the weak 0.18 correlation honestly, but at 98 overlapping points it is hard to read at a glance, which is also why we chose group medians.
  • Title framed as an association (score: -0.5). The title, "Where women's enrollment rose more, the gender gap in labor force participation narrowed more", is true of the group medians and avoids causal verbs. It still invites the reader to infer that education caused the narrowing, which cross-country data cannot show, and "the gap narrowed" does not say whether women rose or men fell. We score this as mildly deceptive, since the wording is literally accurate and the subtitle states that the comparison is between medians. A title stating only that the medians differ by group would have been the more cautious wording and harder to read as causal. It is also flatter and less persuasive as a headline, so we kept the association wording.

Final Reflection

Initially, the supporting side felt like the easier one, because the world numbers already make the case: women's university enrollment roughly tripled whilst their labor force participation slipped from 51% to 48%. The opposing side was harder. In our checkpoint we argued it with women per 100 men by region and with the 15 to 24 age group, and the TA feedback pointed out that we never showed the enrollment trend and that the age chart did not clearly oppose the proposition. The feedback also asked us to use global data instead of a single country such as India. After that, we built both charts on the same 98 countries and territories, so the opposing side could no longer lean on one region that happened to look good. What surprised us most was how much the conclusion depends on the measure of work. Women's own participation rate fell, whereas the ratio of women to men rose, so each side could pick the number that suited it without changing a single value.

We now see ethical analysis and visualization as work where a reader could tell, from the chart and its notes, the main ways a different reasonable analysis would have reached a different conclusion. By that standard, the middle-half bands and the disclosures in this write-up are the earnest parts of our project. It is worth mentioning that most of those disclosures sit beside the charts rather than on them, so a reader who only looks at the image never sees them. Leaving out the ratio from the first chart and women's own rate from the second does not meet this standard. Both charts also treat "gains in work" as joining the labor force, which says nothing about wages, hours or informal work.

For us, acceptable persuasion points the reader at something that is really in the data, for example through emphasis, ordering or a clear headline. None of our deceptive choices needed a truncated axis or a false number: every percentage axis starts at zero and every figure cites its source. The line is crossed when a choice only works if the reader does not know the alternative. Picking a measure, a group or a summary because the obvious option weakens the case is misleading even when every number is correct. Median callouts that make modest gains look like stagnation, a best-case group shown first, and group medians that turn a correlation of 0.18 into a clean upward pattern all fall on that side. A simple test we came up with is whether we would be comfortable showing the reader the version we threw away.
