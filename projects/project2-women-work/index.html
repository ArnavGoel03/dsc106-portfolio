Proposition: Women's gains in education have not translated into gains in work.

Dataset: World Bank, World Development Indicators, 1990 to 2019, fetched from the World Bank Indicators API on 22 October 2026 and cached locally (the cache is not published; running make_charts.py fetches the same indicators again). Indicators: female gross tertiary enrollment (SE.TER.ENRR.FE), female labor force participation, ages 15 and over (SL.TLF.CACT.FE.ZS), and the ratio of the female to the male labor force participation rate (SL.TLF.CACT.FM.ZS).

Sample: the world lines use the World Bank's own world aggregate. The per-country panels in both figures keep only the economies that report all three indicators in some year from 1990 to 2000 and again in some year from 2014 to 2019, and use each one's earliest and latest such years. Of the 187 countries and territories with participation data, 98 pass this test; the other 89 lack enrollment data in one of the two windows and are dropped, including Brazil, Japan, Germany, France, Italy, Nigeria and Australia. The dropped economies are disproportionately high-income (46 of the 89, against 17 of the 98 retained), so the sample leans towards middle-income countries. The 98 include one territory (West Bank and Gaza), so we refer to them as countries and territories.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Enrollment and participation drawn on one percentage axis (score: -1). The left panel places both world series on a single 0% to 60% scale, which invites the reader to compare the rising blue line with the flat brown line directly. The two percentages have different denominators: gross tertiary enrollment is measured against the university-age cohort (and can exceed 100%), whilst participation is measured against all women aged 15 and over, including students and retirees. Placing them on one axis makes the gap look like a failure of one to follow the other, which the data cannot establish. We would justify the shared axis as the conventional way to compare two percentages; two separate panels would be more honest but far less persuasive.
  • Women's own participation rate instead of the female-to-male ratio (score: -1.5). We measure work with the female participation rate and never show the ratio of the female to the male rate. Over the same period the world ratio rose from 66.9 to 68.2, because men's participation fell faster than women's, so by the relative measure women did gain ground. A reader has no way to notice the absence of the alternative measure, which is why we score this as clearly deceptive; it is the mirror image of the measure choice in the opposing chart and receives the same score.
  • Paired histograms of per-country change on one shared axis (score: +1). For each of the 98 countries and territories we compute the percentage-point change in enrollment and in participation, bin both into 5-point intervals, and stack the two histograms over the same x-axis, with zero and the median marked. The persuasion comes from the contrast in spread: the enrollment distribution stretches far to the right whilst the participation distribution stays piled around zero. We score this as earnest, because it shows every retained country rather than chosen examples and prints the median participation gain of one point.
  • A 2-point threshold in the participation annotation (score: -1). The annotation reads "53 of 98 gained under 2 points or fell". Only 40 of the 98 actually fell; the 2-point threshold is our choice, and grouping small gains with losses makes a majority appear to have stalled. We would justify it as a reasonable definition of a negligible change, and the median is printed beside it, but a reader who skims takes away a stronger claim than the numbers support.
  • Title framed on the world aggregate (score: -0.5). The title, "Women's university enrollment tripled; their labor force participation did not rise", is accurate for the world lines (13% to 41%, and 51% to 48%) and uses calm language. It nevertheless frames the whole figure around the world totals, whereas the median country in the right-hand panel gained one point. We score this as only mildly deceptive, since the statement is true and the counter-evidence is printed beneath it.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Opening with the largest-rise group (score: -1). The left panel shows only the 25 countries and territories whose enrollment rose most, where the median enrollment rises from 20% to 72% and the median participation ratio from 55% to 70%. Placing education and work side by side for this group makes the translation from one to the other look direct, and it is the group in which the pattern is strongest. The group is defined in the panel title and matches the tallest bar on the right, so we would justify it as a worked example, but a reader who stops at the left panel sees the best case as if it were typical.
  • The female-to-male participation ratio instead of women's own rate (score: -1.5). We measure work with the ratio of the female to the male participation rate, which rose in most of the 98, and never show women's own participation rate. The ratio is a defensible measure of gender equality and is defined in the footnote, but it can rise simply because men's participation falls, and the world female participation rate declined from 51% to 48% over the same years. Hiding the alternative measure is the same omission as in the supporting chart, so it receives the same score.
  • Quartile bins summarised by their median (score: -1.5). We split the 98 into four equal groups by enrollment gain and plot the median change in the ratio for each, which produces a rising sequence of +1.3, +3.4, +4.3 and +8.8 points. The relationship behind it is weak: the correlation between the two changes is 0.18 (Pearson) and 0.24 (Spearman), and the binning and medians hide almost all of that spread. Both correlations are printed in the footnote and the share of each group that gained is printed above the bars, but the bars themselves are what a reader remembers, so we score this as clearly deceptive.
  • Title framed as a cross-country association (score: -0.5). The title, "Where women's enrollment rose more, women's participation rose more relative to men's", is true of the group medians and avoids causal verbs. It still invites the reader to infer that education caused the gain, which cross-country data of this kind cannot show, and "relative to men's" is easy to read past. We score this as mildly deceptive, since the wording is literally accurate and the subtitle states plainly that the comparison is between medians.

Final Reflection

The supporting chart was the more straightforward of the two to build, because the world figures alone appear to make the case: enrollment roughly tripled whilst participation fell. The difficulty lay in the opposing side. Our checkpoint argued it with the female-to-male participation ratio by region and with a chart of participation at ages 15 to 24, and the TA feedback observed that it did not show the enrollment trend and did not clearly oppose the proposition; the feedback also asked that the supporting side rely on global rather than single-country data. Meeting both requests places the two visualizations on the same 98 countries and territories, and an honest opposing case is considerably harder to make from identical numbers than from a separately chosen region. The most surprising finding was how much the conclusion depends on the measure of work: women's own participation rate fell worldwide, whereas the ratio of the female to the male rate rose, so each side could select the indicator that suited it without altering a single value.

Several of our deceptive decisions are classic techniques: a threshold chosen to group small gains with losses, a best-case group shown first, and four medians that turn a correlation of 0.18 into a clean staircase. What is notable is that none of them required a truncated axis or a false number; every value axis starts at zero and every figure cites its source. It is also worth acknowledging what the indicators cannot capture. Gross tertiary enrollment counts students enrolled, not degrees completed or fields of study, and it includes older and repeating students. Participation for women aged 15 and over counts anyone working or seeking work, so it says nothing about wages, hours, informal employment or seniority, and it is lowered mechanically when more young women are in education. The proposition's "gains in work" is therefore measured only as attachment to the labor market, and both charts inherit that narrow definition.

We now define ethical analysis and visualization as work in which the reader could reconstruct, from the chart and its notes, the main ways in which a different reasonable analysis would have reached a different conclusion. By that standard, the printed medians, correlations and group shares are ethical additions, whereas omitting the ratio from the first chart and women's own rate from the second are not. We would draw the line between acceptable persuasion and misleading design at the point where a choice depends on the reader not knowing about an alternative. Emphasis, ordering and a clear headline are acceptable, because they direct attention to something that is genuinely in the data; a measure, threshold or aggregation chosen because the obvious alternative weakens the conclusion, and then presented without that alternative, is misleading even when every number on the chart is correct. This boundary is not sharp, since every summary discards information, but we believe the test of whether the designer would be comfortable showing the reader the discarded version is a practical one.
