Alpaslan Ozbas, aozbas@ucsd.edu

Bora Vanli, bvanli@ucsd.edu

Yash Goel, a2goel@ucsd.edu

Proposition: Climate-related disasters are becoming more frequent.

Dataset: the IMF Climate Change Indicators Dashboard, table "Climate-related Disasters Frequency", which counts the climate-related disasters recorded each year from 1980 to 2022 in the Emergency Events Database (EM-DAT, maintained by CRED at UCLouvain; the IMF copy was accessed on 31 March 2023). We downloaded the CSV from the dashboard's data service on 22 October 2026 and cached it in a data/ folder that is not part of the published site; running make_charts.py downloads the same file again. The table is wide, with one column per year, so the first step in the script pivots it to one row per country, disaster type and year. Each country has a TOTAL row and up to six type rows (flood, storm, drought, extreme temperature, landslide and wildfire); the six type rows sum exactly to the totals.

Sample: the table covers 215 countries and territories and we keep all of them. The 215 include about two dozen territories and dependencies (for example Hong Kong, Guam, Bermuda and Tokelau) and five former states and territories that only have records for their years of existence (for example East and West Germany, the Soviet Union, Serbia and Montenegro, and the Netherlands Antilles). We treat each row as a reporting unit and refer to them as countries and territories. No row is dropped. We treat empty cells as years in which EM-DAT recorded no disaster: 5,176 of the 9,245 cells in the TOTAL rows are empty and we read every one of them as zero. EM-DAT records events by country, so an event that affects several countries is counted in each. The world totals therefore count country-events, not distinct events.

FOR the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Five-year period means instead of annual counts (score: -1). Each bar is the mean number of disasters per year within a five-year period. That smooths out the single-year peak of 407 in 2005. It also softens the weaker years from 2011 to 2018. So the series reads as a climb with one dip before a new high. In the annual series the count rises until 2000 and then bounces around 340. We kept the 2010-14 dip visible. It is 312 against 356, and that is why the score is only moderate. We also thought about plotting annual counts, but the year-to-year noise hides the long rise.
  • A final period of only three years (score: -1). At first we just used every year the table gave us. So the last bar covers 2020 to 2022. Only three years sit behind it, and its mean is 370. One of those years, 2021, has 391, the second- highest in the table. The bar is drawn at the same width as the five-year bars, and the headline "three times" comes from it. Nothing tells the reader that a three-year mean jumps around more. We also thought about ending at 2019 with a full five-year bar of 322. Honestly, that version probably looks fairer. We dropped it because it cuts the three most recent years and shrinks the headline ratio.
  • Stacked bars showing every disaster type, on an axis from zero (score: +1.5). Instead of going with a single line, we stacked the six types in one muted ramp with floods at the bottom. The axis starts at zero. The reader gets the total and what it is made of. In 1980-84 floods averaged 44 per year. By 2020-22 they were at 200. Storms add much of the rest of the growth. Droughts stay roughly flat whilst landslides only grew from a low base. Nothing here hides a fact that could hurt our case, and we liked that. We also thought about a single total line, but it would hide the split.
  • A fixed panel of consistent reporters (score: -1). After that we worried that the rise came from countries that only started showing up in EM-DAT later. So the right panel repeats the period means for 82 countries and territories. Each one has at least one record in every period. In 1980-84 they averaged 104 per year. By 2020-22 it was 297. Together they hold about 80% of all records. It is still a picked group, and a country can report more events over time without suffering more of them. We were not sure about this one at first, because the panel can look like it closes the coverage question. We also thought about showing only the left panel, but then nobody could answer the coverage objection.
  • Title: "Climate-related disasters are three times as frequent as in the early 1980s, even among consistent reporters" (score: -0.5). Both claims are true of what is drawn. For all countries it is 370 against 122 per year. That works out to 3.0 times. For the 82 consistent reporters the ratio is 2.8 times. That comes from 297 against 104. The wording is calm and the title has no cutoff on the scope. Still, "frequent" suggests real events, and all we have are records. The title also does not say that a three- year mean is compared with a five-year mean. The subtitle does name the averaging, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. We also thought about "more often recorded". We dropped it because it states the claim more weakly than the proposition.

AGAINST the Proposition

Design Decisions and Rationale:

  • Both panels start in 2000, not 1980 (score: -1.5). At first 2000 looked like a fair place to start. But the table begins in 1980, when 104 disasters were recorded. By 2000 the count was 370. The right panel compares the 2000s with the 2010s. It leaves out 2020 to 2022. Those three years have the highest period mean in the table, at 370 per year. A reader sees twenty-three years of ups and downs and none of the twenty years of growth before them. EM-DAT coverage is better after 2000, which is a real argument, but the chart never makes it. Honestly, this one is the hardest to defend, and with equal weighting we think it is the most deceptive choice on this side. We also thought about starting in 1980, but the early rise would dominate the chart and hurt our title.
  • A fitted trend line read as the story (score: -0.5). We went back and forth on adding a dashed least-squares line over the 23 annual counts. Its slope is -0.6 per year. The fit is real and the slope is tiny, which supports "held steady". Still, a line makes the plateau look more exact than it is. The counts range from 287 to 407. The last four years (2019 to 2022) all sit above the line. A fit from 2014 onwards would slope upwards. We drew the raw points next to it, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. We also thought about a moving average, but its shape depends on the window length.
  • Axis from zero to 500 on the annual panel (score: +1). The value axis starts at zero, so the year-to-year swings show at their real size next to the level. A truncated axis from 280 to 410 would make the same series look like a collapse and then a rebound. We did not give it +2. The top of the axis is 500 and the data only need 450, so the line looks a bit flatter than it should. We also thought about a truncated axis, but it would exaggerate the swings.
  • Every country counted once in the decade comparison (score: -1.5). The right panel bins countries by the change in their own decade total. So Romania's fall of 33 and a small island's fall of one each count as one country. Both sit inside the "107 recorded fewer". Of those 107, 57 fell by only one to four. Another 29 had five or fewer disasters in the 2000s. In the 2010s the ten countries with the most records account for 37% of all disasters. Equal weighting does answer how widespread the change was, and the world total also fell, from 3,483 to 3,171. Still, the headline counts are dominated by countries with very few events, and nothing on the chart says so. We also thought about plotting only the change in the world total, but that would hide how many countries moved each way.
  • Title: "The number of climate-related disasters recorded each year has held steady for more than two decades" (score: -0.5). The claim is true of the 23 years shown and the wording is calm. "Recorded" is also more careful than "frequent" in the FOR title. Still, "more than two decades" names a length of time, which makes the window sound like all the history there is. A reader who does not know the earlier years has no reason to ask what came before. The subtitle gives the years, so we scored it as mildly deceptive. We also thought about "since 2000". We dropped it because it makes people ask why the series starts there.

Final Reflection

Initially, we were not sure we could argue both sides of one topic. Our checkpoint used a different proposition (women's education and work), and for the final we picked climate disasters. The supporting chart was the easy one. We had 1,333 recorded disasters in the 1980s. That went up to 2,215 in the 1990s. Then it hit 3,483 in the 2000s. The 2010s were the hard part. They came in at 3,171, below the 2000s, and that was awkward. The opposing chart was hard in a different way. The only version that persuaded was the one that left out the twenty years before 2000. What surprised us most was how much of the debate is about reporting, and how little is about weather. The number of countries with any record in a year roughly doubled between the 1980s and the 2000s.

We went back and forth on how to define ethical visualization. In the end we agreed that a reader should be able to use the chart and its notes to see how a different reasonable analysis would reach a different conclusion. None of our ten decisions needed a truncated axis or an altered number. The problems sit in the shortened window and in the panel that looks like it closes the coverage question. Those choices change what gets counted, so a reader who only sees the image never meets them. Honestly, that bothered us more than any axis trick.

Persuasion turns misleading when a choice only works if the reader does not know the alternative. Emphasis and a confident headline are fine as long as they point at something really in the data. A window or a unit of counting is misleading when we picked it because the other option weakens our conclusion. It is worth mentioning that the indicator counts events and not severity. Whilst thinking about this line, we noticed that better reporting cannot be separated from any change in the climate. The boundary is not sharp.
